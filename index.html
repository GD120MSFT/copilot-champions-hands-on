The pattern

Four ingredients behind every strong prompt

This builds on your enterprise prompting framework. Every prompt today gives Copilot a clear goal, the context, the sources to use, and what a good answer looks like.

GOAL
what to accomplish CONTEXT
audience, situation, time SOURCE
which email, file, meeting EXPECTATIONS
format, length, priorities

Read the pattern in action

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Identify an active initiative I own and prepare a short executive summary for a leadership check-in. The audience is a cross-functional leadership team, the discussion is 15 minutes, and the goal is to align on one decision. Review email threads and meetings from the past two weeks where I am a participant, and cite the sources behind each point. Start with why you chose this initiative, then give three key points, the decision needed, two risks, and a recommended next step.

The colours above are the same four ingredients, highlighted in the prompt itself. Every prompt in this walkthrough carries the strip, and the key ones are colour-coded like this — so you can see the pattern rather than memorise it. That’s what makes it teachable.

  • State the goal.What do you want Copilot to accomplish?
  • Add context.Audience, situation, people, time period.
  • Name the source.The email, meeting, file or data Copilot should use.
  • Set expectations.Format, tone, length, priorities — then verify names and numbers.
Capability 1 · you try this

Personalization & memory

Tell Copilot who you are once, and it persists across every chat — the same AI-first exercise your leaders already ran. Under five minutes.

Try it yourself~5 minCopilot Chat
Two ways in — pick one.

Either answer five quick questions, or let Copilot read your actual work and tell you what it already knows. Both end in the same place: settings that persist across every chat. Start a new chat first.

CHOOSE ONE STARTING POINT

Option A  Have it ask you five questions

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Help me personalize Copilot for my role at Manulife. I want you to respond the same way in every future chat without me re-explaining who I am. Ask me five questions, one at a time: my role and business area, my top priorities this quarter, the people and teams I work with most, the kinds of documents I create, and the tone I prefer. Wait for my answer before asking the next one. Then draft my personalization instructions in the first person, under 200 words, ready to paste straight into Copilot’s settings.

Best when you want full control over what Copilot knows, or your recent work isn’t representative of your actual role.

OR

Option B  Let it read your work first

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Build my Copilot personalization from what you can already see about my work. I want it grounded in evidence rather than guesses, and I do not want a long interview. Use my work profile, anything you already remember about me, and my email, meetings, chats and files from the last 30 days. First give me up to ten short statements about my role, priorities, main collaborators and the documents I produce — and label each one [Profile], [Recent work] or [Assumption]. Then ask me only the two questions you still genuinely need. Then draft my personalization instructions in the first person, under 200 words, ready to paste into Copilot’s settings.

This is the Copilot Premium advantage. It reads your real working context, tells you what it inferred versus what it knows, and only asks what it cannot work out — two questions instead of twenty.

THEN, WHICHEVER YOU CHOSE

Try it  Sharpen it before you save

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Tighten those personalization instructions so they actually change how you answer me. Keep my role, priorities and key collaborators. Add how I want you to handle uncertainty, that I want sources named whenever you use my files or meetings, and my default format — short, structured, decision first. Remove anything generic that would apply to any employee. Return the final version only, ready to paste.

Try it  Prove it works

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Now that my personalization is saved, tell me in one line what you understand about me. Then draft a three-sentence update on what I should focus on this week, based on my recent meetings and email. Point out which parts of your answer came from my personalization — so I can see the difference it made.

If you can’t see the difference, it isn’t specific enough yet. Go back and add what makes your role different from anyone else’s.

  • Start a new chat.So the setup isn’t coloured by earlier context.
  • Pick Option A or Option B.Not both — they’re two routes to the same place. B is the one worth showing your teams.
  • Sharpen it.Cut anything that would apply to any other employee.
  • Save it.Paste the final version into Copilot’s personalization / custom-instructions settings.
  • Prove it works.Run the last prompt — you should be able to see the change.
Leave with it runningDon’t stop at “drafted.” The goal is that your personalization is saved and shaping answers before you leave the room — that’s what you’ll be teaching your teams to do.
Why this matters for championsYou can only teach what you understand. Champions who get the “behind the scenes” of custom instructions and memory can set it up for a whole team.
Capability 2 · you try this

Advanced prompt chaining

One real workflow, end to end — catch up on your week, turn it into a leader update, then into slides — without leaving the flow. Each prompt builds on the last.

Try it yourself~8 minChat → Word → PowerPoint

Try it  1 · Catch up — find the signal

GoalContextSourceExpectations
I am catching up after a busy week and need to walk into my leadership check-in knowing what actually moved. Review my work from the past five business days across email, chats, meetings and files. Identify the five developments most likely to need my attention. For each: what changed, why it matters now, the decision or next step it may need, and the source that supports it. Put the most time-sensitive first, keep each to two or three lines, and separate confirmed facts from things you are inferring.

Try it  2 · Draft the leader update

GoalContextSourceExpectations
From the top three of those, draft a written update for my direct leader, who has fifteen minutes and needs to leave with one decision made. Use the items you just identified as the source. Lead with what needs a decision, then progress, then open questions. Keep it under 200 words, neutral and direct, and keep the sources next to each point.

Try it  3 · Turn it into slides

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Turn the update you just wrote into three slides I will present live to that same leadership group. Use only what is in the update — do not add new claims. One headline decision slide, one progress slide, one risks-and-next-steps slide. Maximum four bullets per slide, under twelve words each, and give me a one-line speaker note per slide.

Notice what makes this a chain. Prompt 2 uses prompt 1’s output as its source, and prompt 3 uses prompt 2’s. You never re-explain the context — that’s the whole technique.

Watch-outVerify before you rely on it. Open at least one cited source. Chaining is powerful precisely because each step trusts the last — so an error in step one travels all the way to your slides.

The same shape, different starting points

The chain you just ran starts from your week. Most repeated work follows the same three-part shape — gather → shape → deliver — it just starts somewhere else. Find yours.

Start from your weekThe one you just ran — for any recurring check-in or status.
Catch up on 5 days→Leader update→Slides
Start from a documentA report, submission or policy lands and something has to come out of it.
Summarize the file→Pull the implications→Draft the response
Start from a meetingDecisions get made and the follow-through is the work.
Recap & decisions→Actions by owner→Follow-up note
Start from dataThe numbers arrive and someone has to explain them.
Analyse the export→Explain what moved→Commentary for review

Try it  Find your own chain

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Look at my work over the last 30 days and find the multi-step outputs I produce again and again — where I gather something, shape it, then deliver it to someone. Give me my top three. For each, show it as a three-step chain, say what starts it off, who receives the final output, and write the first prompt I would run to begin that chain. Then tell me which of the three is the best one to hand to Copilot first, and why.

Ask yourself as you read it back: which of these do I do most often — and what would it be worth if the first draft were already written?

Capability 3 · watch on screen

Cowork — orchestration across your apps

Everything so far, you ran. This is the shift: you describe the outcome and Cowork does the work — creating the documents, drafting the mail, working across your apps, showing each step and asking before it acts. We drive this from the front to stay on time.

Chat — you drive

Single-step, single session. You prompt, it responds, you take the output and do the next thing. Powerful, but every step is yours.

You prompt→It answers→You act
Cowork — it drives

Multi-step and agentic. One instruction, and it works through the task across Outlook, Word, PowerPoint, Teams and your files — pausing for your approval before anything leaves your hands.

You describe→It plans & works→You approve

Not the steps. Say what you need to exist at the end — Cowork works out the sequence.

Watch it work

Presenter demo  The Cowork ask

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Using the attached report as the single source for everything you produce, create a written brief, a short deck and a draft email for my team. The brief is for someone who needs the detail, the deck is for a leadership discussion, and the email is for the team who need to know what to do next. Word brief: under 400 words, covering the situation, the three most important findings with the figure behind each, and a recommended next step. PowerPoint: five slides, no more than four bullets each. Email: under 150 words, decision first, owners and dates second, open questions last. Keep the facts identical across all three — only the framing changes. Leave the email as a draft for me to review; do not send it.

Notice the last line. Cowork will ask before it sends anything — but saying it in the prompt is the habit worth teaching. You stay in control of what leaves your name.

What to look for in the outputThere is a story buried in that report. New business is up 20.8% over the quarter — but one region’s submission-error rate has doubled to 22.4% against a book average of 11.2%, and it is dragging cycle time with it. A good output finds it without being told to.
Where the leverage isCowork can browse your whole Work IQ to pull in what it needs. That’s the difference between an assistant that waits for you to attach a file and one that goes and finds the right file, the meeting it came from, and the thread where it was discussed.
Champion takeawayOne source, three outputs, no drift. Because everything traces back to the same grounded report, the numbers can’t disagree between the brief, the deck and the email — which is exactly what goes wrong when three people build them separately.
Teach the judgement, not just the buttonReach for Cowork when the task is genuinely multi-step and crosses apps. For a quick draft or a fast answer, Chat is still the right tool — and it’s faster. Knowing which to reach for is the champion skill.

Agent Building 2.0

The hard part isn’t how — it’s what

You’ve built an agent before. Today is about spotting the problem worth building one for — then building it, in your pod, in about an hour.

Start from friction

A recurring, annoying, manual moment in your week.

Name the workflow

The repeatable steps a person does today.

Shape the agent

Knowledge, specialty or productivity — pick the pattern.

Before you startBring the friction you most want to hand off. The best agent ideas come from work you already find repetitive — not from a capability you want to try.
The method

Four steps to what’s worth building

Run any idea through these four steps before you touch Agent Builder. This is where the value is — not in the clicking.

  • Start with a friction.A recurring, annoying, manual moment in your week.
  • Name the workflow.What are the repeatable steps a person does today?
  • Find the trigger.What kicks it off — an email, a file, a question?
  • Shape the agent.Knowledge, specialty or productivity — pick the pattern.
Worked example“I re-summarize the same policy documents for every new case.” → a knowledge agent grounded on the policy library that answers case questions in seconds.

Try it  1 · Let Copilot find your friction

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Look across my email, meetings, chats and files from the last 60 days and find the work I repeat. Identify five recurring tasks where I appear to do the same kind of thing more than once a month — the same type of summary, the same status update, the same document, the same question answered for someone else. For each one: what triggers it, roughly how often it happens, the steps I seem to take, and how repeatable it looks on a scale of one to five. Rank them by how worthwhile automating would be, and say what evidence you used.

Start here, not from a blank page. The hardest part of agent building is spotting the right problem — and your own work data already contains the answer.

Try it  2 · Pressure-test the one you picked

GoalContextSourceExpectations
I want to build an agent for this friction: [paste the one you picked]. Act as a sceptical reviewer. Walk it through four questions and answer each one plainly: What are the repeatable steps a person does today? What triggers it — an email, a file, a question, a date? Which of the three patterns fits best: knowledge, specialty, or productivity? And is this genuinely worth an agent, or is it really just a saved prompt? If it is too vague or too varied to automate, say so and suggest a sharper version.
Pick your pattern

Three agent patterns

Almost every useful agent is one of these three. Decide which one your friction needs before you build.

Knowledge

Answers from a trusted source — policy, product, process. Grounded on documents your team relies on.

Specialty

Runs a specific task end to end — against a rubric, framework or standard output.

Productivity

Automates a personal, repeatable weekly chore — the catch-up, the status roll-up.

Decide firstMatch the pattern to the friction. A “where do I find X” problem is a knowledge agent; a “do this the same way every time” problem is a specialty agent.

What this looks like across Manulife

Real examples from the use-case work behind this programme — the same three patterns, in different business groups.

Knowledge

Policy & guideline assistants. Investment guidelines, audit methodology, HR policy, conservation playbooks — grounded on the approved source so the answer is consistent no matter who asks.

Specialty

Drafting against a standard. Variance commentary in the reporting template, risk write-ups in the standard format, campaign briefs against the brand guide — the shape is fixed, only the content changes.

Productivity

The weekly roll-up. Ops status packs, review-pack assembly, meeting follow-through — the chore you do every week that has a predictable trigger and a predictable output.

Notice the pattern behind the pattern. Knowledge agents remove “where do I find it”. Specialty agents remove “how do I write it up”. Productivity agents remove “I have to do this again”. Which of those three is your friction?

Prompts for the build

Copy these as you go. Prompt 1 writes your agent’s instructions; prompts 2–4 are how you test it before the gallery walk.

Try it  1 · Write your agent’s instructions

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Help me write the instructions for an agent in Agent Builder. The workflow is: [paste your friction and its steps]. The people using it are [who]. Draft the agent’s name, a one-sentence purpose, and its instructions written as direct guidance to the agent. Cover what it should always do, what it must never do, what to do when it does not know or the source does not cover the question, and the format its answers should take. Keep the instructions under 250 words and make it clear the output is a draft a human reviews.

Try it  2 · Generate your test questions

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Based on the agent instructions we just wrote, give me six questions to test it with: two an everyday user would obviously ask, two awkward edge cases, one where the answer is genuinely not in the source, and one that tries to push it outside its purpose. For each, tell me in one line what a good answer looks like — so I can judge the response rather than just read it.

This is the step most people skip. An agent that has never been tested against an edge case is a demo, not a tool your team can rely on.

Try it  3 · Fix what the test exposed

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Here is how my agent answered the test questions: [paste the weakest two answers]. Tell me what is wrong with them — too vague, went beyond the source, wrong format, or answered something it should have declined. Then rewrite the specific lines of my agent instructions that would fix it, and show only what changed so I can paste it back in.

Try it  4 · Write the hand-off

GoalContextSourceExpectations
Write a short description of this agent so a teammate can pick it up cold: what it does, who it is for, the exact friction it removes, what it should not be used for, and two example questions to try first. Keep it under 120 words, plain language, no jargon — this goes in our champion channel for other business groups to reuse.
Keep it non-sensitiveUse a fictional or non-sensitive knowledge source for the build. Real, sensitive content comes later once your agent works.
