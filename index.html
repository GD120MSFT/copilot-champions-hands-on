The pattern

RACE + EQ — the anatomy of a strong prompt

This is the RACE + EQ prompting framework. Every prompt today tells Copilot who to be, what to do, what to ground on, what a good answer looks like — and who it is for.

R — ROLE
who Copilot acts as A — ACTION
what you want done C — CONTEXT
the files, emails, meetings to use E — EXPECTATION
what a good output looks like + EQ
tone, audience, style

Read the pattern in action

RoleActionContextExpectationEQ
You are my chief of staff. Identify an active initiative I own and prepare a short executive summary for a leadership check-in. Review email threads and meetings from the past two weeks where I am a participant, and cite the sources behind each point. Start with why you chose this initiative, then give three key points, the decision needed, two risks, and a recommended next step. The audience is a cross-functional leadership team with fifteen minutes and one decision to make — keep it direct and plain-language, no jargon.

The colors above are the same five elements, highlighted in the prompt itself. Every prompt in this walkthrough carries the strip, and the key ones are color-coded like this — so you can see the pattern rather than memorise it. That’s what makes it teachable. Expectation is the one most people skip: they tell Copilot what to do, but never what a good answer looks like.

  • Set the Role.Who should Copilot act as? “You are my chief of staff” changes the answer more than most people expect.
  • State the Action.What exactly do you want done? One clear verb beats three vague ones.
  • Give the Context.The emails, meetings, files or data it should ground on — and ask it to cite them.
  • Define the Expectation.What does a good output actually look like? Structure, length, what to include. This is the most overlooked part.
  • Add the EQ.Who is it for, and how should it land? Tone, audience and delivery — then verify names and numbers.
Capability 1 · you try this

Personalization & memory

Tell Copilot who you are once, and it persists across every chat — the same AI-first exercise your leaders already ran. Under five minutes.

Try it yourself~5 minCopilot Chat
Two ways in — pick one.

Either answer five quick questions, or let Copilot read your actual work and tell you what it already knows. Both end in the same place: settings that persist across every chat. Start a new chat first.

CHOOSE ONE STARTING POINT

Option A  Have it ask you five questions

RoleActionContextExpectationEQ
Act as a Copilot setup coach. Help me personalize Copilot for my role at Manulife, so you respond the same way in every future chat without me re-explaining who I am. Ask me five questions, one at a time: my role and business area, my top priorities this quarter, the people and teams I work with most, the kinds of documents I create, and the tone I prefer. Wait for my answer before asking the next one. Then draft my personalization instructions, under 200 words, ready to paste straight into Copilot’s settings. Write them in the first person, in my voice.

Best when you want full control over what Copilot knows, or your recent work isn’t representative of your actual role.

OR

Option B  Let it read your work first

RoleActionContextExpectationEQ
Act as a Copilot setup coach who works from evidence rather than guesses. Build my Copilot personalization from what you can already see about my work — I do not want a long interview. Use my work profile, anything you already remember about me, and my email, meetings, chats and files from the last 30 days. First give me up to ten short statements about my role, priorities, main collaborators and the documents I produce — and label each one [Profile], [Recent work] or [Assumption]. Then ask me only the two questions you still genuinely need. Then draft my personalization instructions, under 200 words, ready to paste into Copilot’s settings. Write them in the first person, in my voice.

This is the Copilot Premium advantage. It reads your real working context, tells you what it inferred versus what it knows, and only asks what it cannot work out — two questions instead of twenty.

THEN, WHICHEVER YOU CHOSE

Try it  Sharpen it before you save

RoleActionContextExpectationEQ
Act as an editor of my Copilot settings. Tighten those personalization instructions so they actually change how you answer me. Work from the draft you just produced. Keep my role, priorities and key collaborators. Add how I want you to handle uncertainty, that I want sources named whenever you use my files or meetings, and my default format — short, structured, decision first. Remove anything generic that would apply to any employee. Return the final version only, ready to paste. Write it in my voice, in the first person.

Try it  Prove it works

RoleActionContextExpectationEQ
You are my assistant, now running with my saved personalization. Tell me in one line what you understand about me, then draft a three-sentence update on what I should focus on this week. Base the update on my recent meetings and email. Point out which parts of your answer came from my personalization — so I can see the difference it made. Answer exactly the way my settings tell you to, so I can hear the change.

If you can’t see the difference, it isn’t specific enough yet. Go back and add what makes your role different from anyone else’s.

  • Start a new chat.So the setup isn’t colored by earlier context.
  • Pick Option A or Option B.Not both — they’re two routes to the same place. B is the one worth showing your teams.
  • Sharpen it.Cut anything that would apply to any other employee.
  • Save it.Paste the final version into Copilot’s personalization / custom-instructions settings.
  • Prove it works.Run the last prompt — you should be able to see the change.
Leave with it runningDon’t stop at “drafted.” The goal is that your personalization is saved and shaping answers before you leave the room — that’s what you’ll be teaching your teams to do.
Why this matters for championsYou can only teach what you understand. Champions who get the “behind the scenes” of custom instructions and memory can set it up for a whole team.
Capability 2 · you try this

Advanced prompt chaining

One real workflow, end to end — catch up on your week, turn it into a leader update, then into slides — without leaving the flow. Each prompt builds on the last.

Try it yourself~8 minChat → Word → PowerPoint

Try it  1 · Catch up — find the signal

RoleActionContextExpectationEQ
You are my chief of staff. I am catching up after a busy week — identify the five developments most likely to need my attention. Review my work from the past five business days across email, chats, meetings and files. For each: what changed, why it matters now, the decision or next step it may need, and the source that supports it. Put the most time-sensitive first, keep each to two or three lines, and separate confirmed facts from things you are inferring. I am walking straight into a leadership check-in, so keep it skimmable and direct.

Try it  2 · Draft the leader update

RoleActionContextExpectationEQ
You are my chief of staff. From the top three of those, draft a written update for my direct leader. Use the items you just identified as the source, and keep the sources next to each point. Lead with what needs a decision, then progress, then open questions. Keep it under 200 words. My leader has fifteen minutes and needs to leave with one decision made — neutral and direct, no padding.

Try it  3 · Turn it into slides

RoleActionContextExpectationEQ
You are my chief of staff. Turn the update you just wrote into three slides. Use only what is in the update — do not add new claims. One headline decision slide, one progress slide, one risks-and-next-steps slide. Maximum four bullets per slide, under twelve words each, and give me a one-line speaker note per slide. I am presenting these live to that same leadership group, so keep the wording tight enough to say out loud.

Notice what makes this a chain. Prompt 2 uses prompt 1’s output as its source, and prompt 3 uses prompt 2’s. You never re-explain the context — that’s the whole technique.

Watch-outVerify before you rely on it. Open at least one cited source. Chaining is powerful precisely because each step trusts the last — so an error in step one travels all the way to your slides.

The same shape, different starting points

The chain you just ran starts from your week. Most repeated work follows the same three-part shape — gather → shape → deliver — it just starts somewhere else. Find yours.

Start from your weekThe one you just ran — for any recurring check-in or status.
Catch up on 5 days→Leader update→Slides
Start from a documentA report, submission or policy lands and something has to come out of it.
Summarize the file→Pull the implications→Draft the response
Start from a meetingDecisions get made and the follow-through is the work.
Recap & decisions→Actions by owner→Follow-up note
Start from dataThe numbers arrive and someone has to explain them.
Analyze the export→Explain what moved→Commentary for review

Try it  Find your own chain

RoleActionContextExpectationEQ
Act as a workflow analyst looking at how I actually spend my time. Find the multi-step outputs I produce again and again — where I gather something, shape it, then deliver it to someone. Give me my top three. Look at my work over the last 30 days across email, meetings, chats and files. For each, show it as a three-step chain, say what starts it off, who receives the final output, and write the first prompt I would run to begin that chain. Then tell me which of the three is the best one to hand to Copilot first, and why. Keep it practical — I want to act on this today, not read an analysis.

Ask yourself as you read it back: which of these do I do most often — and what would it be worth if the first draft were already written?

Presenter demo  The Cowork ask

RoleActionContextExpectationEQ
You are my chief of staff. Create a written brief, a short deck and a draft email for my team. Use the attached workbook as the single source for everything you produce. Word brief: under 400 words, covering the situation, the three most important findings with the figure behind each, and a recommended next step. Work out the findings from the data rather than repeating totals back to me. PowerPoint: five slides, no more than four bullets each. Email: under 150 words, decision first, owners and dates second, open questions last. Keep the facts identical across all three — only the framing changes. The brief is for someone who needs the detail, the deck is for a leadership discussion, and the email is for the team who need to know what to do next — match the tone to each.

Notice the last line. Cowork will ask before it sends anything — but saying it in the prompt is the habit worth teaching. You stay in control of what leaves your name.

What to look for in the outputThere is a story buried in that data. New business is up sharply over the quarter — but one region’s submission-error rate has roughly doubled to about 23% against a book average near 11%, and it is dragging cycle time and service load with it. A good output finds that on its own, without being told to look.
Where the leverage isCowork can browse your whole Work IQ to pull in what it needs. That’s the difference between an assistant that waits for you to attach a file and one that goes and finds the right file, the meeting it came from, and the thread where it was discussed.
Champion takeawayOne source, three outputs, no drift. Because everything traces back to the same grounded report, the numbers can’t disagree between the brief, the deck and the email — which is exactly what goes wrong when three people build them separately.
Teach the judgment, not just the buttonReach for Cowork when the task is genuinely multi-step and crosses apps. For a quick draft or a fast answer, Chat is still the right tool — and it’s faster. Knowing which to reach for is the champion skill.

Agent Building 2.0

The hard part isn’t how — it’s what

You’ve built an agent before. Today is about spotting the problem worth building one for — then building it, in your pod, in about an hour.

Start from friction

A recurring, annoying, manual moment in your week.

Name the workflow

The repeatable steps a person does today.

Shape the agent

Knowledge, specialty or productivity — pick the pattern.

Before you startBring the friction you most want to hand off. The best agent ideas come from work you already find repetitive — not from a capability you want to try.
Pick your pattern

Three agent patterns

Almost every useful agent is one of these three. Decide which one your friction needs before you build.

Knowledge

Answers from a trusted source — policy, product, process. Grounded on documents your team relies on.

Specialty

Runs a specific task end to end — against a rubric, framework or standard output.

Productivity

Automates a personal, repeatable weekly chore — the catch-up, the status roll-up.

Decide firstMatch the pattern to the friction. A “where do I find X” problem is a knowledge agent; a “do this the same way every time” problem is a specialty agent.

What this looks like across Manulife

Real examples from the use-case work behind this programme — the same three patterns, in different business groups.

Knowledge

Policy & guideline assistants. Investment guidelines, audit methodology, HR policy, conservation playbooks — grounded on the approved source so the answer is consistent no matter who asks.

Specialty

Drafting against a standard. Variance commentary in the reporting template, risk write-ups in the standard format, campaign briefs against the brand guide — the shape is fixed, only the content changes.

Productivity

The weekly roll-up. Ops status packs, review-pack assembly, meeting follow-through — the chore you do every week that has a predictable trigger and a predictable output.

Notice the pattern behind the pattern. Knowledge agents remove “where do I find it”. Specialty agents remove “how do I write it up”. Productivity agents remove “I have to do this again”. Which of those three is your friction?

The method

Four steps to what’s worth building

Run any idea through these four steps before you touch Agent Builder. This is where the value is — not in the clicking.

  • Find the trigger.What kicks it off — an email, a file, a question?

Try it  1 · Let Copilot find your friction

RoleActionContextExpectationEQ
Act as a workflow analyst. Find the work I repeat. Identify five recurring tasks where I appear to do the same kind of thing more than once a month — the same type of summary, the same status update, the same document, the same question answered for someone else. For each one: what triggers it, roughly how often it happens, the steps I seem to take, and how repeatable it looks on a scale of one to five. Start here, not from a blank page. The hardest part of agent building is spotting the right problem — and your own work data already contains the answer.

Prompts for the build

Copy these as you go. Prompt 1 writes your agent’s instructions; prompts 2–4 are how you test it before the gallery walk.

Try it  1 · Write your agent’s instructions

RoleActionContextExpectationEQ
Act as an agent designer. Help me write the instructions for an agent in Agent Builder. The workflow is: [the friction you noted above] — [the steps you noted above]. It is triggered when [your trigger], and it should be a [your pattern]. The people using it are [who it is for]. Draft the agent’s name, a one-sentence purpose, and its instructions written as direct guidance to the agent. Cover what it should always do, what it must never do, what to do when it does not know or the source does not cover the question, and the format its answers should take. Keep the instructions under 250 words. Write it so a colleague could read it and understand what the agent is for, and make clear the output is a draft a human reviews.

Try it  2 · Generate your test questions

RoleActionContextExpectationEQ
Act as a sceptical tester of my agent. Give me six questions to test it with. Base them on the agent instructions we just wrote. Two an everyday user would obviously ask, two awkward edge cases, one where the answer is genuinely not in the source, and one that tries to push it outside its purpose. For each, tell me in one line what a good answer looks like — so I can judge the response rather than just read it. Phrase them the way a real colleague would ask, not as formal test cases.

This is the step most people skip. An agent that has never been tested against an edge case is a demo, not a tool your team can rely on.

Try it  3 · Fix what the test exposed

RoleActionContextExpectationEQ
Act as a sceptical reviewer of my agent. Tell me what is wrong with these answers, then rewrite the specific lines of my agent instructions that would fix it. Here is how my agent answered the test questions: [paste the weakest two answers]. Diagnose each one — too vague, went beyond the source, wrong format, or answered something it should have declined. Then show only the instruction lines that changed, so I can paste them back in. Be direct — I would rather fix it now than defend it later.

Try it  4 · Write the hand-off

RoleActionContextExpectationEQ
Act as the person documenting this agent so others can reuse it. Write a short description of this agent so a teammate can pick it up cold. Base it on the agent we just built and tested. Cover what it does, who it is for, the exact friction it removes, what it should not be used for, and two example questions to try first. Keep it under 120 words. Plain language, no jargon — this goes in our champion channel for other business groups to reuse.
Keep it non-sensitiveUse a fictional or non-sensitive knowledge source for the build. Real, sensitive content comes later once your agent works.
